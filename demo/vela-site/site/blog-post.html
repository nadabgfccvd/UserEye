Scaling observability to 10 trillion events a day

TW By Tomas Weiser · August 18, 2026 · 12 min read

On this page

  1. The wall at 4 trillion
  2. Going columnar
  3. The Rust query core
  4. Results
  5. What we'd do differently

For two years our ingestion pipeline was a source of pride. A Go service fanned out to Kafka, a consumer tier enriched spans, and a heavily-tuned time-series database stored the result. It carried us from 50 billion to 4 trillion events per day. Then, in March 2026, it stopped.

Not catastrophically — worse. It degraded the way only distributed systems can: ingest lag crept from 2 seconds to 40, queries that used to take 800 ms started timing out at 30 s, and our storage bill grew 3× faster than event volume. The architecture hadn't broken. It had succeeded into a corner.

The wall at 4 trillion

Profiling showed the bottleneck wasn't CPU or network — it was write amplification. Every span touched four indexes. Every index wrote a WAL. Every WAL got compacted on a schedule that didn't care about our traffic shape. At 46 million events per second, we were spending 71% of IOPS re-writing data we had already written.

The expensive part of observability is not storing your data once. It is storing it five times in five different shapes “just in case”.

Going columnar

The re-architecture boiled down to one decision: store each event exactly once, in a columnar layout, sorted by time and service. Everything else — indexes, rollups, aggregates — became lazily materialized views computed at query time or by background workers, never on the ingest hot path.

Three details did the heavy lifting:

  1. Sort keys that match questions. We sort by (timestamp, service, route) because 94% of queries filter on exactly those. Sort order is the cheapest index you will ever buy.
  2. Dictionary encoding for everything repetitive. Service names, routes, status codes and regions become 16-bit dictionary IDs. Compression improved 2.4× and scans got faster because the CPU touches less memory.
  3. Late materialization. Filters run on encoded columns; the expensive wide-row assembly happens only for rows that survive.

The Rust query core

Storage was half the battle. The other half was a query engine that could scan billions of encoded values per second. We wrote it in Rust with SIMD-accelerated filter kernels, and — this matters more than any micro-optimization — with a benchmark harness that runs on every pull request against a fixed 500 GB corpus.

-- the query that used to time out, now ~380 ms
SELECT route,
       quantile(duration_ms, 0.95) AS p95,
       count(*) AS requests
FROM traces
WHERE service = 'checkout-api'
  AND timestamp >= now() - INTERVAL '24 hours'
GROUP BY route
ORDER BY p95 DESC
LIMIT 10;

Results

Six months after cutover, at 10.2 trillion events per day:

  • p99 query latency: 3.1 s → 1.9 s (and the median is 380 ms)
  • ingest lag: 40 s → 1.4 s at peak
  • storage cost per event: −63%
  • IOPS spent on write amplification: 71% → 9%

What we'd do differently

Three honest regrets. First, we'd model the sort key decision as a product question from day one, not an infra one — query patterns are the product. Second, we'd invest in the benchmark corpus before the rewrite, not during; two regressions slipped through the early weeks. Third, we'd migrate tenant-by-tenant instead of region-by-region — the blast radius of a bad week would have been 2% of customers, not 30%.
